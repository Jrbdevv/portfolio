BigBattery: CuringAnalysis Paralysis

In my first six months at BigBattery I took on the biggest gaps in the customer journey, starting with a catalogue so tangled that buyers couldn't tell which battery fit their system. That work cleared the way for something bigger: a product line, backed by hundreds of thousands of dollars, built to bring affordable, long-lasting LiFePO4 storage to market.

The Brief

BigBattery sells lithium storage into six verticals at once: residential, commercial, marine, RV, golf and off-grid. Half the buyers are electricians who want a spec sheet. The other half have never sized a battery bank in their life. The catalogue had grown to 180+ product pages serving both, and it was buckling under itself.

Capacity variants each had their own page, so eight listings competed for the same search term and diluted the authority of all eight. Product pages bounced at 53.3%. Seven in ten carts were abandoned at the final step. In an 850-person survey, 44% could not find the product they wanted and 47% could not understand the one they found. Across session recordings, sales call logs and survey responses, the same sentence kept surfacing: I can't tell which battery is right for my system.

That is not a copy problem. A buyer who cannot self-qualify either calls the sales team or leaves, and most leave.

Phase 01 / Find the real failure

I wanted the diagnosis to come from evidence, not opinion, so I triangulated behaviour, attribution and the customer's own words before touching a wireframe.

  • 931 Hotjar sessions. Recordings showed a consistent shape: land, scroll straight past the spec table, never reach a CTA. Bounce sat at 53.3%, and the plan only sampled 17% of traffic, so Microsoft Clarity came in to capture every session, with heatmaps and quick-back tracking.
  • Triple Whale attribution traced where every customer came from (organic search and SEO rankings, Meta and Google ads, backlinks, Klaviyo email) against a 5.82 blended ROAS, and flagged new-customer acquisition cost climbing 31%.
  • 850-respondent survey, mixed methods, splitting "couldn't find it" (44%) from "couldn't understand it" (47%), which are two different fixes.
  • Stakeholder interviews with the CEO and the sales team, who named checkout confusion as the top driver of inbound support calls.
  • Competitive teardown, which found the pattern we were missing: discovery organised by application rather than by product family, with specs progressively disclosed instead of front-loaded.

The conclusion was that the site was organised around BigBattery's internal taxonomy and a buyer's mental model starts somewhere else entirely: with the job.

Phase 02 / Collapse the catalogue

Consolidation came first, because every later decision multiplied across however many pages existed. Near-duplicate listings were competing with each other for the same search term and the same buyer, so I restructured the catalogue around one parent page per product family, with configurations selected inline. 180+ pages became 30. SEO authority consolidated, and buyers stopped guessing which listing was the real one.

In Figma I mapped the catalogue page by page: which listings differed by nothing but battery quantity, and which kit page each one would fold into. That map became the System Configuration Modal. A buyer swaps the inverter or the battery in place instead of waiting for a new page to load on every click, so the site feels faster and more responsive, and the whole product base reads as one connected system at a glance.

Navigation was rebuilt around application entry points rather than internal categories, and product families gained dual-application tagging after testing surfaced a real gap: hardware suited to both grid-tied and off-grid use was invisible in either single-category search.

The configuration model took six passes to land. V1 through V5 worked through an inverter selector, a combined battery-and-inverter page, grid and off-grid segmentation, and a restructured below-fold, before V6 shipped in December 2025 carrying multiple inverter and battery configurations on one parent page.

Phase 03 / Lead with the outcome, not the spec

Every page was re-sequenced to open with what the product is for ("for off-grid homes", "for solar backup") and to release technical depth on demand. Specs moved below the fold in a scannable structure, so experts can still get to them in one scroll while a first-time buyer is not asked to interpret a voltage table to qualify themselves.

The trade-off was immediate: technical buyers genuinely preferred specs at the top. I resolved it with a persistent spec summary bar, so the numbers stay reachable while the page still leads with the outcome.

The area below the fold became a decision engine in five layers: visual explanations and infographics rather than walls of text, installation and real-use video, downloadable spec sheets and wiring diagrams, side-by-side comparison tables, and warranty and policy surfaced instead of buried in a footer. Trust signals moved up next to the add-to-cart, on the argument that a battery is a high-consideration purchase and warranty, compatibility and shipping are pre-purchase anxieties, not post-purchase details. Only the three highest-converting signals made the cut, since each one costs visual clarity.

Phase 04 / Build a system, not more pages

Every new page had previously been designed from scratch, which produced visual inconsistency that quietly undermined trust and slowed development to a crawl. I built a shared library instead: product cards in grid, list and comparison variants; an application-led mega menu; spec tables that collapse on mobile; trust strips; a three-tier CTA hierarchy; and use-case modules.

It shipped as documentation rather than a folder of frames: Figma variables so a global change cascades, autolayout and responsive behaviour spelled out, dev-ready links, interaction specs, and the usage context for each component. Foundations covered brand guidelines, type scales, spacing tokens, grid rules and component states.

The same logic drove the visual work. Energy products share a structure, so rather than drawing one-off graphics I built modular infographic templates: system diagrams tracing panel to controller to battery to inverter to load, capacity comparisons showing what a given bank actually runs and for how long, and per-vertical use-case scenarios. That came to 42+ assets deployed across product pages, landing pages, Meta and Google creative, and Klaviyo lifecycle email, with one system feeding every channel.

Selling this internally meant dropping the UX vocabulary. A component library does not make any single page visibly better, so the case was made in development hours saved and pages shipped per sprint.

Phase 05 / Ship, measure, repeat

Weekly reviews with the CEO and CTO kept every decision framed in conversion and revenue terms rather than design principle. Post-launch, HYROS carried attribution while Hotjar carried behaviour, and the loop stayed open rather than closing at launch.

It kept earning its keep. Heatmaps caught rage clicks on infographic banners, because users expected them to be clickable, so they got hover states and CTA overlays. Post-redesign checkout recordings showed users completing without the hesitation loops that had defined the old flow, and full journeys from homepage banner through deals page, product page, cart and checkout were confirmed completing as designed.

Afterwards

The catalogue work opened onto a post-purchase problem. Husky 2, a Bluetooth companion app for LiFePO4 systems, answers the question owners were calling support to ask: is my system healthy right now. It reports state of charge, voltage, current draw and temperature, with per-cell voltage across all 16 cells and five discrete temperature sensors for anyone who wants that depth.

Two decisions carried the design. It reports time rather than percentage, since "full in 15 min" is actionable in a way that "75%" is not, and it works over Bluetooth alone, because the off-grid and marine users who need it most have no Wi-Fi to depend on. Multi-battery setups are addressed by Module IDs over CAN and RS485, which doubles as the upsell path into a larger system.

If I were extending this, two things deserve more than they got: structured testing with B2B buyers specifically, whose decision cycles are longer and more consultative than the survey captured, and deeper design-system documentation, which is functional today but could be a genuine accelerant with real investment.
